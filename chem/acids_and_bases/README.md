# Acids and Bases

**Level:** High school, Advanced Chem 2 (AP Chemistry)
**AP unit:** Unit 8, Acids and Bases, worth 11–15% of the multiple-choice section. Only Unit 3 (18–22%) is weighted more. Acid–base questions also show up regularly in the free-response section, usually built around a titration.
**AP Chemistry exam:** Thursday, May 6, 2027, afternoon session. Confirm the date with the school's AP coordinator.

The outline follows the College Board's *AP Chemistry Course and Exam Description* (the fall 2024 edition, which is current). Topic numbers like **8.3** match that document and AP Classroom. In the fall 2024 edition Unit 8 has 11 topics. Older review books show 10, because 8.11, pH and Solubility, used to be topic 7.13 in Unit 7.

## How to use this folder

Each topic folder has two files:

- **`notes.md`** has the key ideas, worked examples, and common mistakes.
- **`practice.md`** has AP-style problems. Solutions are hidden under **Show solution** so you can try each problem first.

Work through the folders in order, since each builds on the last. If a step in the notes doesn't make sense, look back at the folder it depends on. Your answers may differ from the solutions in the last digit because of rounding. That's fine.

**Before you start:** Unit 8 is Unit 7 (equilibrium) applied to acids. Make sure these feel comfortable first:

- writing a K expression, and knowing that pure liquids and solids are left out (7.3–7.4)
- what a large or small K means (7.5)
- ICE tables and the small-x approximation (7.7)
- Q vs. K, and Le Châtelier's principle (7.9–7.10)
- Ksp and the common-ion effect (7.11–7.12), which you'll need for folder 08

### 1. pH and strong acids and bases (8.1, 8.2)
- Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases, conjugate pairs, and amphoteric species (water)
- Autoionization of water: Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C
- pH, pOH, and pH + pOH = 14.00 at 25 °C; converting between pH and concentration
- Kw depends on temperature: neutral water has pH below 7 when it's warm, yet it is still neutral
- The six strong acids to memorize, plus the group 1 and group 2 hydroxides
- [OH⁻] is twice the concentration for group 2 hydroxides like Ba(OH)₂
- Significant figures for logs: decimal places in pH = sig figs in the concentration

### 2. Weak acids and bases (8.3)
- Ka and Kb expressions; pKa and pKb; a smaller Ka means a weaker acid
- ICE tables for a weak acid or a weak base, and the small-x shortcut with the 5% check
- Percent ionization, and why it *increases* when a weak acid is diluted
- Ka × Kb = Kw for a conjugate pair, so the weaker the acid, the stronger its conjugate base
- Predicting whether a salt solution is acidic, basic, or neutral

### 3. Acid–base reactions and mixtures (8.4)
- **React first, then equilibrate.** Do the stoichiometry in moles, then decide which equilibrium sets the pH
- Strong + strong: the excess reagent sets the pH
- Weak acid + strong base, or weak base + strong acid: the result is a buffer, an excess of the strong reagent, or the conjugate alone, depending on the mole ratio
- Weak + weak: an equilibrium with K = Ka(acid) / Ka(conjugate acid of the base)
- Writing net ionic equations, with strong acids as ions and weak acids as molecules

### 4. Titrations (8.5)
- Vocabulary: analyte, titrant, equivalence point, endpoint, and half-equivalence point
- At equivalence, moles of titrant = moles of analyte, which lets you find an unknown concentration
- **At half-equivalence, pH = pKa**, which lets you find Ka from a curve
- The pH at equivalence is 7 (strong/strong), above 7 (weak acid), or below 7 (weak base)
- The four regions of a weak acid curve and the method for each
- Polyprotic acids: count the jumps to get the number of acidic protons, and name the major species in each region (qualitative only)
- Lab error analysis: how a mistake changes the calculated concentration

### 5. Molecular structure and acid strength (8.6)
- Strong acids have very weak, stabilized conjugate bases
- Electronegativity, inductive effects, and resonance stabilize the conjugate base and make the acid stronger
- Oxyacid trends (HClO < HClO₂ < HClO₃ < HClO₄) and carboxylic acids
- Which hydrogen is acidic: the O–H in carboxylic acids, not the C–H
- Common weak bases: ammonia, amines, and carboxylate ions

### 6. pH, pKa, and indicators (8.7)
- pH < pKa means the acid form (HA) dominates; pH > pKa means the base form (A⁻) dominates; at pH = pKa they are equal
- Each pH unit away from pKa means a 10× change in the ratio
- Indicators are weak acids whose two forms have different colors; pick one whose pKa is close to the equivalence-point pH

### 7. Buffers (8.8, 8.9, 8.10)
- A buffer is a large amount of *both* members of a conjugate pair
- Net ionic equations for how a buffer neutralizes added acid or base
- Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]); choose an acid with pKa close to the target pH
- Buffer capacity: more concentrated means more capacity; a ratio that favors A⁻ handles added acid better, and one that favors HA handles added base better

### 8. pH and solubility (8.11)
- Salts with basic anions (F⁻, CO₃²⁻, OH⁻, PO₄³⁻, CH₃COO⁻) become more soluble as pH drops
- Salts of strong-acid anions (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, NO₃⁻) are not affected by pH
- Explain the effect with Le Châtelier's principle (qualitative only)

## What the AP exam will *not* ask

The official course description lists these exclusions for Unit 8. Advanced Chem 2 tests may still include some of them, so the practice sets include a few, labeled **Beyond the AP exam**.

- Calculating the concentration of each species along a **polyprotic** titration curve. Qualitative reasoning about which species are present is still tested. (8.5)
- Calculating the **change in pH when acid or base is added to an existing buffer**. You still need the pH of a weak acid partly neutralized by a strong base, which is part of 8.4. (8.9)
- Deriving the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation. (8.9)
- Calculating solubility as a function of pH. (8.11)

## Given on the formula sheet vs. what to memorize

**Given on the exam:** Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ and pKw = 14 = pH + pOH (both at 25 °C), pH = −log[H₃O⁺], pOH = −log[OH⁻], the Ka and Kb expressions, pKa = −log Ka, pKb = −log Kb, Kw = Ka × Kb, pKw = pKa + pKb, and pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]).

**Not given, so memorize:**
- The strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HClO₄, H₂SO₄ (first proton only), HNO₃
- The strong bases: group 1 hydroxides (LiOH, NaOH, KOH, …) and group 2 hydroxides (Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂)
- That pH = pKa at half-equivalence
- The 5% rule for the small-x approximation
- To go from pH back to concentration: [H₃O⁺] = 10^(−pH)

**About the exam:** 60 multiple-choice questions in 90 minutes (50% of the score) and 7 free-response questions in 105 minutes (50%). The free-response section has 3 long questions worth 10 points each and 4 short ones worth 4 points each. A calculator is allowed on both sections. The exam is "hybrid digital": you answer multiple choice in the Bluebook app and handwrite free-response answers in a paper booklet, so practice writing out full solutions by hand.
