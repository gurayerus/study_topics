**1.** For each salt, predict whether its solubility increases or stays about the same when the solution is made more acidic.

(a) AgCl (b) CaF₂ (c) BaCO₃ (d) PbBr₂ (e) Mg(OH)₂ (f) Ca₃(PO₄)₂

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

| Salt | Anion | Effect of acid |
|---|---|---|
| (a) AgCl | Cl⁻, from a strong acid | **about the same** |
| (b) CaF₂ | F⁻, a weak base | **increases** |
| (c) BaCO₃ | CO₃²⁻, a weak base | **increases** |
| (d) PbBr₂ | Br⁻, from a strong acid | **about the same** |
| (e) Mg(OH)₂ | OH⁻, a strong base | **increases** |
| (f) Ca₃(PO₄)₂ | PO₄³⁻, a weak base | **increases** |

</details>

**2.** Which change would increase the solubility of Zn(OH)₂(s) in water?

(A) adding NaOH  (B) adding HNO₃  (C) adding Zn(NO₃)₂  (D) adding more Zn(OH)₂(s)

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**(B).** H₃O⁺ from HNO₃ reacts with OH⁻ (H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2 H₂O), removing it from Zn(OH)₂(s) ⇌ Zn²⁺ + 2 OH⁻. The equilibrium shifts right, so more dissolves.
- (A) and (C) add a common ion (OH⁻ or Zn²⁺), which *decreases* solubility.
- (D) Adding more solid doesn't change the equilibrium.

</details>

**3. (Free-response style)** A marble statue (CaCO₃) slowly dissolves when exposed to acid rain.

(a) Write the equation for the dissolution equilibrium of CaCO₃(s).
(b) Write the net ionic equation for the reaction between carbonate ions and H₃O⁺.
(c) Explain, using Le Châtelier's principle, why acid rain increases the amount of CaCO₃ that dissolves.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

(a) CaCO₃(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq)

(b) CO₃²⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) → HCO₃⁻(aq) + H₂O(l). With more acid, the HCO₃⁻ reacts further to H₂CO₃, which breaks down into CO₂ + H₂O.

(c) H₃O⁺ from the acid rain reacts with CO₃²⁻, **lowering [CO₃²⁻]**. With a product ion removed, Q < Ksp, so the dissolution equilibrium **shifts right** and more CaCO₃ dissolves.

</details>

**4.** Tooth enamel is mostly hydroxyapatite, Ca₅(PO₄)₃OH. Fluoride treatments replace some of the OH⁻ with F⁻, forming fluorapatite, Ca₅(PO₄)₃F.

(a) Explain why acids produced by mouth bacteria dissolve hydroxyapatite.
(b) Explain why fluorapatite holds up better against acid than hydroxyapatite does.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

(a) Hydroxyapatite releases OH⁻ and PO₄³⁻ ions, both of which are bases. H₃O⁺ from the acids reacts with them (making H₂O and HPO₄²⁻), removing them from the solution. The dissolution equilibrium shifts right, so the enamel dissolves.

(b) F⁻ is a much **weaker base** than OH⁻: its Kb is only about 10⁻¹¹, while OH⁻ is a strong base. In mildly acidic conditions like those in the mouth, much less F⁻ than OH⁻ gets removed by H₃O⁺. The dissolution equilibrium shifts much less, so fluorapatite dissolves less.

</details>

**5.** A student adds a few drops of concentrated NaOH to a saturated solution of Mg(OH)₂ in contact with solid Mg(OH)₂. What happens to the amount of solid, and to [Mg²⁺]? Explain.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**More solid forms, and [Mg²⁺] decreases.** OH⁻ is a common ion. Adding it raises Q above Ksp for Mg(OH)₂(s) ⇌ Mg²⁺ + 2 OH⁻, so the equilibrium shifts left and Mg(OH)₂ precipitates. (This is topic 7.12, the common-ion effect, applied to a hydroxide.)

</details>
