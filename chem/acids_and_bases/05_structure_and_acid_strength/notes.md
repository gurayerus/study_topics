# 5. Molecular structure and acid strength

AP topic **8.6 Molecular Structure of Acids and Bases**

> **Goal:** Use molecular structure to explain why one acid is stronger than another, and to pick out which hydrogens are acidic.

This topic is about argumentation, not calculation. Free-response questions ask you to **justify** a claim using structure.

## The big idea: look at the conjugate base

```
HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻
```

An acid is strong when its conjugate base A⁻ is **stable**, meaning comfortable carrying the negative charge. The more stable A⁻ is, the further the equilibrium lies to the right, and the larger Ka is.

So for any comparison, ask: **which conjugate base handles the negative charge better?** Four structural features stabilize it.

## 1. Electronegativity of the atom holding the H (same row)

Across a period, a more electronegative atom holds a negative charge better and makes a more polar X–H bond.

```
acidity:   CH₄  <  NH₃  <  H₂O  <  HF
```

## 2. Size of the atom (going down a group)

For the hydrogen halides, acidity **increases** going down the group, even though electronegativity *decreases*:

```
acidity:   HF (weak, Ka ≈ 7 × 10⁻⁴)  <  HCl  <  HBr  <  HI   (all three strong)
```

A larger atom forms a longer, weaker bond to H, and it spreads the negative charge over a larger volume. HF is the classic trap: F is the most electronegative element, yet HF is a weak acid, because the H–F bond is very strong.

## 3. Inductive effect (electronegative atoms pulling electron density)

Electronegative atoms near the acidic O–H pull electron density toward themselves through the bonds. This weakens the O–H bond and spreads out, and so stabilizes, the negative charge on the conjugate base.

**Oxyacids with the same number of O atoms:** the more electronegative the central atom, the stronger the acid.

| Acid | Central atom | Approx. pKa |
|---|---|---|
| HOCl | Cl (EN 3.2) | 7.5 |
| HOBr | Br (EN 3.0) | 8.6 |
| HOI | I (EN 2.7) | 10.6 |

**Carboxylic acids with Cl substituents:** each Cl pulls more electron density away from the COOH group.

| Acid | Approx. pKa |
|---|---|
| CH₃COOH | 4.74 |
| ClCH₂COOH | 2.9 |
| Cl₂CHCOOH | 1.3 |
| Cl₃CCOOH | 0.7 |

## 4. Resonance (spreading the charge over several atoms)

**Oxyacids of the same element:** more O atoms make a stronger acid.

```
acidity:   HClO  <  HClO₂  <  HClO₃  <  HClO₄ (strong)
```

In ClO₄⁻, the negative charge is shared by resonance over four O atoms. In ClO⁻ it sits on one. The extra O atoms also add an inductive pull.

**Carboxylic acids vs. alcohols:**

| | Acetic acid, CH₃COOH | Ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH |
|---|---|---|
| pKa | 4.74 | ≈ 16 (about as weak an acid as water) |
| Conjugate base | acetate, CH₃COO⁻ | ethoxide, CH₃CH₂O⁻ |
| Charge | shared by two O atoms (resonance) | stuck on one O atom |

```
          O                    O⁻
         //                   /
   CH₃ – C          ↔   CH₃ – C          (the two structures are equivalent,
          \                    \\         so the charge is split between both O atoms)
           O⁻                   O
```

**Carboxylic acids (–COOH) are the most common class of weak acid** you'll see on the exam.

## Which hydrogens are acidic?

- In oxyacids and carboxylic acids, the acidic H is bonded to **O**.
- **C–H hydrogens are not acidic.** Carbon isn't electronegative enough, and the C–H bond is barely polar.
- Acetic acid, CH₃COOH, has four H atoms but is **monoprotic**: only the O–H hydrogen is acidic.
- Phosphorous acid, H₃PO₃, is a classic trick question. Its structure is HPO(OH)₂, with one H bonded directly to P, so it's only **diprotic**.

## Bases and structure

- **Nitrogen bases:** the lone pair on N accepts a proton. Examples are ammonia (NH₃) and amines like CH₃NH₂:

  ```
  CH₃NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃NH₃⁺ + OH⁻
  ```

- **Carboxylate ions** (CH₃COO⁻, HCOO⁻) are weak bases, the conjugates of weak acids.
- **Strong bases** (group 1 and 2 hydroxides) supply OH⁻, whose conjugate acid, H₂O, is extremely weak.
- **Strong acids** (HCl, HBr, HI, HClO₄, H₂SO₄, HNO₃) have **very weak conjugate bases** (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, ClO₄⁻, HSO₄⁻, NO₃⁻), stabilized by electronegativity, size, induction, resonance, or a combination.

## How to write the justification (free response)

A full-credit answer usually has three parts:

1. **Claim:** "HClO₃ is a stronger acid than HClO."
2. **Structural feature:** "HClO₃ has two more O atoms bonded to Cl."
3. **Link to the conjugate base or the O–H bond:** "The extra electronegative O atoms pull electron density away from the O–H bond and delocalize the negative charge of ClO₃⁻ by resonance, so ClO₃⁻ is more stable than ClO⁻. A more stable conjugate base means the acid ionizes more, giving a larger Ka."

Vague answers like "because it has more oxygens" or "because Cl is electronegative" usually lose the point. Always say **what the feature does** to the bond or the conjugate base.

## Common mistakes

- **Using electronegativity alone for HF vs. HCl.** Down a group, bond strength and size win.
- **Counting all the H atoms as acidic.** CH₃COOH is monoprotic.
- **Mixing up "stronger acid" and "stronger conjugate base."** These go in opposite directions.
- **Not mentioning the conjugate base.** Most AP rubrics reward reasoning about its stability or the polarity of the O–H bond.
